Why Your Toddler Wants the Same Song on Repeat, Again and Again.

By the Zaydio team · Music by Bradley Schachter · Published July 29, 2026
Last updated August 10, 2026

It's 7:43am. You've heard the same song eleven times since breakfast started. Your toddler just said "again!"

Here's the thing that might save your sanity: that eleventh play isn't wasted on them. It might be the one where a new word sticks.

The study that vindicates every "again!"

Researchers at the University of Sussex ran an experiment that maps almost perfectly onto your morning. They read 3-year-olds storybooks containing new made-up words. One group heard different stories each session; the other group heard the same stories repeated. Both groups got the exact same number of exposures to the new words.

Right after reading, both groups of kids seemed similar, but just a week later, only the repeated-story kids still knew the words. The variety group had forgotten them to the point where it seemed like they had never heard the words at all (Horst, Parsons & Bryan, 2011, Frontiers in Psychology).

Same words. Same amount of exposure. The only difference was repetition of the context — and it was the difference between learning and forgetting.

Why repetition works when variety doesn't

Repetition works because familiarity frees attention: once a song's melody is automatic, a toddler's brain can spend its effort on the words instead. To you, play number eleven is identical to play number one. To your toddler, it isn't. The first few times through a song, they're grabbing the melody and the general shape. Once that's familiar, their brain stops spending effort on it, which frees them to notice the words. Then what the words mean. Then the little joke in verse two you assumed they missed.

Familiarity isn't the absence of learning. It's the platform for it. Each repeat lets them build on what's already solid instead of starting over. When everything is new every time, nothing gets built on. This is what happened to the variety group in the study.

There's a bonus finding that music-video parents will appreciate: in an earlier experiment, preschoolers who watched the same episode of a kids' show five times understood it far better than kids who saw it once (Crawley et al., 1999). The loop is a feature — and it's one of the few things that actually shrinks the screen-learning gap, which we unpack in our honest take on toddler screen time.

The comfort part is real too

Knowing a song completely soothes because, for toddlers, predictability is power. It's not all vocabulary. A song your toddler knows completely is a small world where they can predict everything. They know what comes next, when the funny part hits, how it ends. For a person whose entire life is unpredictable new things, that's genuinely soothing. Knowing what comes next isn't boring to a toddler. It's power.

So when they demand the song again, they're doing two things at once: consolidating what they're learning, and taking a break in a place where they're the expert.

What to actually do (besides surrender)

Let the streak run. The repetition is the mechanism. You don't need to inject variety for their sake. The urge to rotate is yours, not theirs.

Change your job, not the song. By play twenty, stop performing and start playing: leave gaps for them to fill, get the words wrong so they can correct you (toddlers live for correcting you), ask what comes next. Same song, escalating game. (This is the same trick from our post on whether animal sounds count as words — the pause is an invitation. And if you're self-conscious about the singing itself, here's why you don't have to sing well.)

Ride the phase out. Song obsessions burn bright and end on their own, usually replaced without ceremony by the next one. You'll hear the old song months later and feel weirdly nostalgic. This is normal and there is no cure.

Protect your own ears strategically. Headphones for kids aren't the answer at this age. The singing-along is where the good stuff happens, but you're allowed a "Mom/Dad gets one grown-up song per morning" rule. Modeling that other music exists is its own tiny lesson.

The honest caveat

Loving repetition is universal toddler behavior. If repetition is showing up alongside other things on your mind, such as big reactions to tiny changes in routine, or fewer words than you'd expect, it's something worth mentioning to your pediatrician as their own conversation. The song on loop by itself isn't a flag. It's just a normal Tuesday.

Quick answers

Is it normal for my toddler to want the same song over and over?

Completely. Repetition is one of the main ways toddlers learn. Research shows children retain new words far better from repeated material than from varied material with the same total exposure.

Should I let my toddler listen to the same song on repeat?

Generally yes. The repeats are doing real work: familiar material frees attention for new details. Sing along and vary the interaction rather than the song.

How long do toddler song obsessions last?

Anywhere from days to months. They typically end on their own when a new favorite takes over.

Does repetition help toddlers learn words?

Yes! In one study, only children who heard the same stories repeatedly still remembered new words a week later; children who heard varied stories forgot them entirely.
