You Don't Have to Sing Well. Really.

By the Zaydio team · Music by Bradley Schachter · Published August 17, 2026
Last updated August 10, 2026

Somewhere in the last seven posts on this blog, we've told you to sing along about thirty times. Sing with the video. Sing in the car. Sing the Spanish song at bath time. Leave a gap and let them fill it.

And we know you must be asking: “Sure, easy for you to say, but have you heard me sing?”

We know, we get it.

The short version: your toddler does not care. The longer version is better, because the research doesn't just excuse your singing voice. It says your singing voice, exactly as it is, is the one your child wants most.

Babies go quiet for singing

Babies attend longer — and go still — for maternal singing than for speech. In a University of Toronto lab, researchers showed 6-month-olds videos of their own mothers, once talking to them and once singing to them. The babies watched the singing longer. They locked in, and they went still while they did it; that intense frozen attention babies do when something puts them in a trance-like state (Nakata & Trehub, 2004).

Nothing about those mothers was auditioning for anything. They were just moms, singing the way moms sing. And it outperformed their talking on every measure of attention the researchers had.

Singing also does something talking can't: it regulates. Across studies, infant-directed singing beats infant-directed speech at keeping babies calm and holding off distress. In one experiment, singing kept babies content roughly twice as long as talking did. The steady pulse and repetition of a song seem to settle a small nervous system in a way that speech, with all its unpredictability, doesn't. (For a review of musical affect regulation in infancy, see Trehub, Ghazban & Corbeil, 2015.)

Here's the part that should end the embarrassment

Strangers can tell baby-directed singing by its warmth, not by vocal skill. Researchers recorded mothers singing the same song twice: once to their baby, once to their older child. Then they played the versions to strangers. The strangers could reliably pick out the baby version. Not because it was better sung. Because it sounded more loving (Bergeson & Trehub, 1999).

And here's the detail we love: the baby-directed versions had worse enunciation. Sloppier words. The mothers weren't performing more carefully for their babies, they were performing more warmly, and the warmth was the je ne sais quoi everyone could hear.

Read that again as a nervous singer: the measurable active ingredient in singing to your child is not pitch, not tone, not hitting the note. It's the love in the delivery. Which means the thing you're self-conscious about and the thing your child is responding to are two completely different channels. You're worried about the music. They're listening to you.

There's one more layer. Your voice isn't just a voice to your child, it's the most familiar sound in their world, tied to safety since before they were born. When that voice sings, it's not competing with professional singers. It's in a category professional singers can't enter.

You are in a league of your own.

So what are our videos for, then?

Recordings teach songs; your voice supplies the familiarity recordings can't. Fair question, coming from us. Honest answer, same as always: recordings genuinely work — research finds recorded songs hold babies' attention and keep them content far better than speech does. Our job is to write songs worth repeating and sing them well enough that they stick.

But your voice does something ours can't. We can't be familiar. We can't be the voice that's held them at 3am. The best version of a Zaydio song isn't the one in the video, it's the one where the video taught you the song, and now it's coming out of you in the car with the windows down and the key entirely wrong.

Think of it as a duet where we handle the backing track and you handle the part that matters — the same idea behind the video deficit, explained.

The permission slip, in practical form

Sing quietly if you're shy. Volume is optional. Proximity isn't. Close and quiet beats loud and far every time.

Steal our pitch. If starting a song from nothing feels exposed, sing with the recording. You get the melody scaffolding; your kid still gets your voice. Training wheels are allowed forever. (Same idea as teaching a toddler a second language with songs: the recording supplies the scaffold, you supply the human.)

Let them correct you. When you crack on a note, your toddler will either not notice or find it the funniest thing that's happened all day. Both outcomes are wins. (Toddlers living to correct you is a feature of the science of toddler repetition.)

Remember it works on you too. Studies of new parents found that singing to their baby lowered the parents' stress. The song is co-regulation: it settles both people in the room. You're not performing. You're both calming down.

And if you truly won't sing: hum. Humming is singing with the door closed, and the research on melody, pulse, and your familiar voice applies just the same.

The Zaydio confession

The person who writes and sings our songs has been making music for 45 years. He's good. And if you asked him whose voice your toddler most needs to hear singing these songs, he'd say the same thing the research says: not his. Yours.

That's the actual design of everything we make. Our songs are meant to be learned from us and taken from us. Sung worse, and better, by you.

Quick answers

Should I sing to my baby if I can't sing?

Yes. Research shows babies respond to the emotional warmth of a familiar voice, not vocal skill. In studies, the versions of songs directed at babies were identifiable by how loving they sounded, not how well they were sung.

Do babies prefer their parents' singing to recordings?

Recordings work well for attention and calming. But a parent's voice carries something recordings can't: familiarity and security built from birth. The two aren't competitors. Recordings teach the songs; parents give them their power.

Does singing off-key confuse or harm a child's musical development?

No. Young children absorb melody, rhythm, and language from all the music around them. An off-key parent singing warmly does far more good than silence.

Why does singing calm babies better than talking?

Singing's steady pulse and repetition help regulate a baby's arousal, while speech is more variable. Studies find infant-directed singing keeps babies content significantly longer than speech.
